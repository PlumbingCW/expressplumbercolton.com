Why Colton's very hard water keeps killing heating elements

.

The City of Colton's own water quality report lists total hardness for the Colton/Rialto supply at 150 to 230 mg/L, with an average of 206.67 mg/L. Divide by 17.1 and that is roughly 12 grains per gallon. On the USGS scale, anything above 180 mg/L counts as very hard, so the average sits in the top band.

Hard water leaves calcium and magnesium scale on whatever is hottest. In an electric heater, scale coats the heating element and acts like a blanket. The element can no longer shed heat into the water, so it runs hotter than it was built to and burns out early. In a gas tank, scale and sediment pile up on the bottom, between the burner flame and the water, which slows heating and makes the steel run hot.

That is why a repeat element failure or a heater that rumbles and pops is often a scale problem, not bad luck. Replacing a part without addressing the scale can mean the same call a second time. Ask the technician to show you the old element or the drained sediment.

Where a leaking water heater is actually leaking from

.

Water on the floor near a heater does not always mean the tank has failed. The source decides whether this is a small repair or a replacement.

The tank body

Water weeping from the steel shell or the bottom seam means the inner tank has corroded through. Nothing sold as a repair fixes a hole in the tank, so this one leads to replacement.

Inlet and outlet fittings

Drips at the top connections come from worn flex connectors, dielectric nipples or loose unions. These are real repairs, and the water often runs down the tank and looks like a body leak.

The relief valve and its pipe

A temperature and pressure relief valve opens when heat or pressure runs too high. A steady drip from the discharge pipe can mean a worn valve, high house pressure, or a tank that has nowhere for expanding water to go.

The drain valve

Plastic drain valves crack and brass ones seep at the threads, especially after a tank has been drained and the valve has been worked loose. A new valve is a quick fix when this is the only source.

Condensation

Cold water filling a tank can sweat on the shell, and some high-efficiency and heat pump units produce condensate by design. Dry the area and watch it. A puddle that returns only after hot showers is usually not a leak.

Garage and closet installs

A heater in a closet or garage can leak for days before anyone sees it, because the water soaks drywall, a wood sill or the slab edge. A drain pan with a line outside is the early warning, so check whether yours has one.

No hot water: the checks that separate a quick repair from a dead heater

.

A cold shower is the most common reason people call us urgently about a heater. The first split is fuel. On an electric unit, the usual culprits are a tripped breaker, a tripped high-limit reset on the thermostat, a failed upper element, or a failed thermostat. A dead lower element alone gives you a short supply of hot water that runs out quickly.

On a gas unit, we look at whether the pilot or igniter is working, whether the thermocouple or flame sensor is sensing flame, whether the gas control valve is opening, and whether the flue is drawing. If you smell gas at any point, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before you call us.

We test heater components and thermostats. Panel and house wiring problems belong to a licensed electrician, and we will tell you plainly when a no-heat call turns out to be one of those.

What a repair visit checks, in order

.

A diagnosis that skips steps tends to replace the wrong part.

  1. Find the source of the water or the cold. We dry the heater, run a paper towel along every joint, and trace drips upward to the highest wet point before touching anything.
  2. Read the data plate. The plate shows fuel type, capacity, model and serial number. The serial number tells us the age, and the model decides which parts exist.
  3. Test the controls. For electric units we check power at the thermostats and resistance across each element. For gas units we check ignition, flame sensing, gas supply and venting.
  4. Check pressure and the expansion path. A gauge on a hose bib shows house pressure. High pressure or a closed system with no room for expansion can stress valves and fittings and cause repeat leaks.
  5. Look inside when it matters. If the tank rumbles or heats slowly, we drain a few gallons to see sediment and scale. What comes out tells us whether a flush is worth doing or the tank is past it.
  6. Give you the options with a price. You hear what failed, what a repair costs, and whether replacement makes better sense. The written price comes before work starts.

What decides the price of a water heater repair

.

Water heater repairs range from a single replaceable part to a job that is really a tank swap in disguise. The factors below explain why two similar calls can end up at different prices. If a leak has already soaked a wall or floor, leak detection can confirm whether the heater is the only source.

Which part failed. A drain valve, thermostat, element, igniter, gas control valve and relief valve all differ in part cost and in labor to reach them.
Fuel type and model. Gas, electric and heat pump units use different parts. Some manufacturer parts are easy to source and others take an order.
Access and location. A heater in a tight closet, an attic or a garage corner takes longer to work on than one in the open.
Water damage and cleanup scope. If water has spread, the leak repair is only part of the job. Drying and drywall work are separate trades and we say so.
Time of day. After-hours and weekend calls are priced for the dispatch, and you hear that before we start.
Homeowners policies often cover sudden and accidental water damage and often exclude wear, corrosion or slow leaks, so a heater that has been seeping for weeks may not qualify. Read your policy, take photos of the damage and the heater before cleanup, and keep any failed parts.
You get a clear written price before work starts, and no work begins until you agree.

Gas, electric and heat pump heaters fail in different places

.

Gas tank heaters fail at the burner assembly, the igniter or thermocouple, the gas valve and the flue. Their repair cost depends on whether a gas component or a simple sensor failed. Electric tanks fail at elements and thermostats, which are inexpensive parts, so an electric repair is usually the more repairable of the two.

Hybrid or heat pump water heaters add a compressor, a fan, an evaporator coil and a condensate drain on top of the electric elements. In general terms, they need open air around them, a place for condensate to go, and clean air filters. Error codes are common causes of calls, and the code is the first thing to write down.

Heat pump units use their own diagnostics and often sealed parts, so repairs follow the manufacturer's service procedure. Where a part is under warranty, we point you to the manufacturer first. If the unit needs the electrical supply changed, that is a licensed electrician's work. If you are weighing a different type of heater, water heater installation covers the choices.

Water heater leaking or no hot water right now: what to do before we arrive

.

A leaking heater can flood a closet or garage in an hour, and a heater that will not heat can mean a family without hot water tonight. Our 24/7 line takes water heater calls day and night, and same-day appointments are available when schedules allow.

Do these things while you wait. They protect the house and make the repair faster.

  • Close the cold water supply to the heater. The shutoff valve on the pipe above the tank stops the refill. If it will not turn, close the main water valve for the house.
  • Turn off the power or the gas control. Switch the breaker off for an electric unit. For gas, turn the control to pilot or off. A hot tank with no water in it can be damaged.
  • Keep people away from wet electrical areas. Do not stand in water near the heater, outlets or a panel. If water has reached an outlet, leave the breaker alone and wait.
  • Smell gas? Leave first. Walk out, then call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or relight a pilot.
  • Photograph the heater and the damage. Take pictures of the data plate, the leak and any wet materials for your insurer before you mop.
