How to Install a Kitchen Sink and Garbage Disposal: A Colton, CA Homeowner Guide

.
By the Colton Express Plumbing team · Updated October 24, 2026
.

To install a kitchen sink and garbage disposal, set and seal the sink, mount the disposal's flange and mounting ring under the drain opening, hang the unit, connect the discharge to the trap, and leak test. A Colton homeowner with basic tools can do the disposal in a few hours. The sink itself is a bigger job.

This guide lists the tools, the steps in order, and the mistakes that cause leaks. It also tells you when to hand the job to a licensed plumber. For repairs to an existing unit, see our garbage disposal repair page.

Tools and parts to lay out before you shut the water off

.

Gather these first, because the water stays off while you hunt for a missing part.

  • Hand tools. Adjustable wrench, channel pliers, screwdrivers, a hammer, and a flashlight or headlamp.
  • Sealant. Plumber's putty for the sink flange, and silicone caulk for the sink rim if the sink instructions call for it.
  • Disposal kit. Check that the box includes the mounting assembly, the gasket, and a power cord, or buy a cord kit if the unit is hardwired.
  • Drain parts. A new trap, a tailpiece, and a discharge elbow. Old slip nuts and washers often fail when reused.
  • Cleanup gear. A bucket, rags, and a dry towel to lay inside the cabinet for the leak check.

The sink first: setting a drop-in or undermount basin

.

Work from the top down, and measure twice, because a cutout is hard to take back.

  1. Shut off and disconnect. Close both supply valves, open the faucet to relieve pressure, then remove the old drain, trap, and clips. Cut any caulk with a utility knife before lifting the old sink.
  2. Check the cutout. Trace the new sink template and compare it to the opening. Trim only if the countertop material allows it, and ask the fabricator before cutting stone or solid surface.
  3. Mount the faucet and strainer. Install the faucet on the sink before it goes in, since the space under the counter is tight. Set the basket strainer with a ring of putty.
  4. Seat the sink. Apply sealant to the rim, lower the sink, and tighten clips evenly from the underside. Wipe squeeze-out right away.

Hanging the disposal: flange, mounting ring, and the knockout plug

.

This is where most leaks begin, so take it slowly.

  1. Set the sink flange. Press a rope of putty under the flange lip and push it into the drain opening. From below, slide on the gasket, backup ring, and mounting ring, then snap the retaining ring into place.
  2. Tighten the mounting screws. Turn the three screws a little at a time so the flange pulls down level. Trim the squeezed putty from the top with a plastic scraper.
  3. Knock out the dishwasher plug. If a dishwasher drain hose will connect to the disposal, remove the plug in the side inlet first. Insert a screwdriver and tap it out, then fish the plastic disc out of the grinding chamber.
  4. Hang the unit. Lift the disposal onto the mounting ring, line up the tabs, and rotate until it locks. Keep the discharge outlet pointing toward the trap.
  5. Connect the discharge and drain. Bolt on the discharge elbow with its gasket, then run the pipe to the trap. The drain should slope gently toward the wall with no sag where water could pool.

Leak points and fixes after the first test

.

Run water into the sink and watch each joint on a dry towel before you close the cabinet.

Drain alignment: the quiet cause of slow drains and leaks

.

A disposal outlet sits at a fixed height, and the trap must meet it without forcing pipes sideways. If the drain stub in the wall is too high or too low, you may need an extra elbow or a trap adapter. Forcing a misaligned pipe tight may hold on day one and leak by week two.

Keep the horizontal run short and sloped, and do not tie into the wall stub with a steep upward bend. Water that stands in the trap carries food scraps and starts the smell people blame on the disposal itself.

Final checks before you flip the switch

.

Power and water should both be proven safe before the first grind.

  1. Confirm the power source. A plug-in unit needs a grounded outlet under the sink. A hardwired unit needs its wires connected inside the junction box, which is electrical work. We do not do electrical work, so hire an electrician if that part is unclear.
  2. Run cold water first. Turn the supply back on, fill the basin, and let it drain while you look for drips.
  3. Test with the disposal on. Run cold water, switch the unit on for ten seconds, and watch every joint again.
  4. Recheck in an hour. Wipe each joint dry and check once more. Slow seeps often take a while to show.
