Snaked it and it came back: why repeat clogs return

.

A cable cuts a channel through a blockage. It does not scrub the pipe wall. If grease, scale or sludge lines the pipe, the opening is still narrow and the next load of waste catches again. That is why a drain can run well for two weeks, then slow once more.

Roots work the same way. A cutter removes the part inside the pipe, but fine root hairs stay in the joint and regrow. Jetting can wash those fibers out and flush the debris downstream, which a cable alone leaves in place.

If your pattern is repeat clogs in the same line, the cause is probably the wall of the pipe or a defect in it, not the last item that went down. The camera tells us which.

Signs a line needs scouring and not another snaking

.

These patterns point to build-up along the pipe wall.

A clog every few weeks

The same drain slows on a regular cycle. That rhythm usually means a coating narrows the pipe until it closes.

Gurgling across several fixtures

Air moving through a partly blocked main shows up in toilets and tubs. It is a sign of reduced capacity and not just one clog.

Greasy kitchen line

Restaurants and busy households pour fat down the sink. Cooling fat forms a hard layer that cables do not remove.

Slow flow after a cable visit

If water drains but never at full speed, the plug is gone and the coating is not.

Roots from a nearby tree

Hair-fine root growth entering at joints is common in older clay and cast iron lines and tends to recur.

What a high-pressure water jet actually does inside a pipe

.

A jetting hose ends in a nozzle with several small openings. Most face backward to pull the hose forward. A few face forward or outward to cut through the plug and strike the pipe wall at an angle. As the hose is pulled back, the spinning spray scours the whole circumference.

The water then flushes loosened material downstream to the sewer main. That is the advantage over a cable, which leaves the loosened debris to settle. Different nozzles suit different jobs, from a rotating cutter for roots to a wide spray for grease.

The method does not remove everything. Hard mineral deposits can resist the spray, and a heavy root mass may need a cable first to open a path. A technician chooses the order, sometimes cable then jet.

How a jetting visit runs, from camera to final check

.

The sequence matters because jetting should never be the first look at an unknown pipe.

  1. Camera inspection. A camera on a flexible rod goes in through the cleanout. It shows the pipe material, the position of the clog and any cracks, offsets or sags.
  2. Choose the access point. Jetting needs a clear opening large enough for the hose. Most jobs use the main cleanout, and some use a removed toilet or a roof vent.
  3. Match the nozzle. The technician picks a nozzle for grease, scale or roots and starts at a modest pressure so the pipe is not stressed.
  4. Jet and flush. The hose advances through the line, then is pulled back while the water scours the wall. Repeated passes clear heavier build-up.
  5. Verify with the camera. A second look confirms the wall is clean and shows what the cleaning revealed, such as a crack that was hidden under debris.

What drives the cost of a jetting job

.

Jetting involves a larger machine, more water and more setup than a hand auger, so it is priced differently. Several factors move the number.

Length and size of the line. A short branch is quick. A long run to the street takes multiple passes and more time.
Access point. A cleanout makes setup simple. If the hose must go through a removed fixture or a roof vent, that adds steps.
Severity of build-up. Light grease may need one pass. A pipe nearly closed with scale can need several passes and more than one nozzle.
Camera work. Looking before and after helps avoid damage and documents the result, and it adds time.
Time of day. Calls at night and on weekends involve after-hours staffing, which affects what the visit involves.
Homeowners policies often cover sudden and accidental water damage and often exclude wear, corrosion or slow leaks. Routine cleaning is usually a maintenance cost. If a backup damaged your home, read your policy and photograph the damage before cleanup.
A clear written price comes before work starts, and the camera finding that supports it is shown to you.

Pipe condition limits and honest alternatives

.

Jetting is not safe on every pipe. A line that is cracked, offset at the joints, collapsed or made of brittle old material can be damaged by water pressure, and the spray can force water out through gaps into the soil. This is the main reason we do not jet blind.

If the camera shows damage, we say so and explain the options. A cable pass may open the line for now. A localized fix can repair one section. A longer fix can reline or replace the pipe, which our sewer line repair page describes in detail.

Some jobs do not need jetting at all. A single hair clog in a tub responds to a hand tool, and our drain cleaning page covers routine clearing. We would rather recommend the smaller job when it fits.

Backup in the house right now: what to do before jetting begins

.

A line that is backing up into floor drains, tubs or toilets is urgent. Call the 24/7 line, and ask for a same-day or after-hours appointment. Jetting after hours is available when conditions allow it, and the camera still comes first.

  • Stop all water use. Do not run the washer, dishwasher, shower or toilets. More water only adds to the overflow.
  • Leave the cleanout capped. A full line can spill from an open cleanout. Let the technician open it with the right equipment.
  • Find the cleanout. It is often a capped pipe near the foundation, in a side yard or by the garage. Clear plants and boxes away from it.
  • Protect the lowest drain. Water backs up at the lowest opening first. Move belongings off the floor near a shower, tub or floor drain.
  • Avoid chemicals. Drain openers in a flooded line can burn skin and do not help jetting.
